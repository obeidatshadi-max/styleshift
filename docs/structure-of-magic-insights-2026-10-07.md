## The book is about therapy, not sales

Every application to pharma reps below is **my adaptation**, not a claim the authors make. The authors stress respect for the client's "integrity" (p. 42) and not forcing them into behaviours "which he himself has not largely set up" (Polster & Polster, quoted p. 42). Any StyleShift feature built on this should keep the same spirit: questions that help the doctor be precise, never verbal tricks to corner them.

## Core ideas (pp. 1–66)

### 1. The map is not the territory (Ch. 1, pp. 7–13)
People don't act on the world directly; they act on a *model* of it, filtered by neurological, social (language) and individual constraints. No two people's models are the same.

### 2. People aren't "bad, crazy, or sick" — their model has too few choices (p. 14)
Behaviour "makes sense when it is seen in the context of the choices generated by their model." The problem is not wrong choices but *not enough* choices.
→ **For reps:** a doctor's objection makes sense inside the doctor's model. The job is to understand and widen that model, not to argue it down. (This backs StyleShift's existing `argued_with_doctor` penalty.)

### 3. Three universal modelling processes (pp. 14–16)
- **Generalization** — one experience comes to stand for a whole category (the rocking-chair child, p. 15).
- **Deletion** — attending to some parts of experience and leaving others out. Example: a man convinced he wasn't worth caring for literally did not hear his wife's caring messages (p. 15).
- **Distortion** — reshaping sensory data. When those messages were pointed out, he reinterpreted them: "She just says that because she wants something" (p. 16).

Together they form a **self-fulfilling loop** (p. 16): the generalization filters and distorts new evidence, so it is never challenged.
→ **For reps:** a doctor who has concluded "this drug doesn't work for my patients" may not register new data, or may reinterpret it. Repeating the data louder won't help; recovering the specific experience behind the generalization might.

### 4. Surface Structure vs Deep Structure (Ch. 2, pp. 27–36)
What someone says (surface) is a reduced and reshaped version of the fuller meaning (deep). Pieces get **deleted** ("Tripod ate" ← "Tripod ate something"), reordered, or **nominalized** — a process turned into a thing ("Susan knows her fear of her parents", p. 32).

### 5. Three options when something is missing: accept, ask, or guess (pp. 41–42)
- Accepting the vague version makes progress slow.
- **Asking** for the missing piece is the direct route: "I'm scared." → "Of what?"
- **Guessing** is fine *with a safeguard*: have the person say the guessed sentence and check whether it fits ("I want you to try saying this and see whether it fits for you…", p. 42).
→ **For reps:** a tentative paraphrase the doctor can accept or correct ("So it's mainly your elderly patients who stop?") is the sales version of that safeguard.

### 6. "Who, specifically?" — nouns with no referential index (pp. 47–48)
"People", "everyone", "some of my family" don't point at anyone specific. The therapist keeps asking *who, specifically?* until a real referent appears ("My father scares me").
→ **For reps:** "Patients don't tolerate it." → "Which patients, specifically?"

### 7. "How, specifically?" — unspecified verbs (pp. 48–49)
"My father scares me" still doesn't say *how*. The question: "How does your father scare you?" Exercise A (pp. 58–59) shows that with a vague verb like *hurt*, the listener invents the details. Learn to separate **what the speaker said** from **what you filled in**.
→ **For reps:** "It didn't work." → "What did you see happen?" Notice when you're assuming what the doctor meant.

### 8. Nominalizations: frozen processes (pp. 43–44)
"I really regret my decision" turns ongoing *deciding* into a finished *event*. The fix is to turn it back into a process the person can still influence, e.g. ask "what stops you from reconsidering?"
→ **For reps:** "My decision is made." / "We had a bad experience." — these are processes stated as closed events.

### 9. "What stops you…?" and "What would happen if…?" (pp. 50–51)
For statements of impossibility ("I can't trust people"), the authors call **"What stops you from…?"** "crucial" for reconnecting someone with what they left out.
→ **For reps:** "I can't switch stable patients." → "What stops you?" / "What would happen if you did, for a new patient?"

### 10. Hold back advice until the model can take it (p. 50)
**Probably the most directly useful insight for StyleShift.** Suggestions that fall into the gaps of someone's model are "relatively ineffective" — the person will "resist" or "not hear" them. Keep suggestions until the model is rich enough to take them in.
→ **For reps:** this is the case against the **premature pitch**, from a completely different field. It backs the existing `premature_pitch` and `product_first_opening` penalties.

### 11. Ask for "how", not "why" (p. 56, footnote 11)
The authors find it effective to ask for **how (process)** answers rather than **why (justification)** answers.
→ This matches StyleShift's existing `forbidden_reason` rule ("why are you prescribing what you currently prescribe?" just makes the doctor rehearse their reasons). The book gives that rule a second, independent rationale.

### 12. Cause-effect ("X makes me feel Y") (pp. 51–52)
"My husband makes me mad" assigns your own response to someone else. Challenges: does it happen *every* time? What's different on the occasions it doesn't?
→ **For reps:** "Side effects make patients stop." → "Every patient? What's different about the ones who continue?" *(an adaptation; the book's example is about emotions)*

### 13. Presuppositions — the negation test (pp. 52–53)
Whatever must be true for a sentence to make sense, *and* for its negation to make sense, is a hidden assumption. "I realize that my wife doesn't love me" presupposes she doesn't — and so does "I don't realize…". Hidden assumptions are "particularly insidious" because they aren't offered up for discussion.
→ **For reps:** "When the price comes down I'll look at it" presupposes price is the deciding factor. Worth surfacing, gently.

### 14. Comparatives with a missing term (p. 66)
"She is better for me" — better *than what*? One term of the comparison has been deleted.
→ **For reps, both ways:** a doctor's "Drug X is better" (than what? for which patients?) and, importantly, **the rep's own claims** ("better tolerated", "more effective", "the safest option"). An unanchored comparative from a rep is a weak claim and possibly a compliance issue.

### 15. "Well-formed in therapy" checklist (p. 54)
A statement is fully clear when it has no unexplored deletions, no nominalizations, no words without a specific referent, no unspecified verbs, no unexplored presuppositions, and no semantic violations (like cause-effect over someone else's feelings). This gives a clear checklist for "is this doctor statement specific enough to act on?"

### 16. Practise until the new choice is yours (p. 53)
New choices must be "actually exercised, practiced" (the authors cite psychodrama, homework, tasks). That is what StyleShift's drills already are.

### 17. The book teaches by exercise (pp. 3, 57–66)
The authors insist on drills ("USE THE EXERCISES", p. 3): sentence lists to classify (complete vs. deleted), then write the fuller version, then write the recovery question. That three-step format maps directly onto a drill.

## Feature ideas for StyleShift

Ranked by value for effort. Each points to the code it would extend.

### A. Analyst behaviours for precision questioning — *small, low risk*
Add proposed behaviour keys to `src/scoring/scoring.config.json`, which `src/agents/behaviorAnalyst/prompt.ts` picks up automatically:
- `discovery.specifying_question` (+) — asked the doctor to make a vague statement specific (who/which/what/how specifically, "compared with what?") *(insights 6–7, 14)*.
- `discovery.what_stops_question` (+) — asked what stops the doctor, or what would happen if… *(insight 9)*.
- `active_listening.checked_interpretation` (+) — offered a tentative reading and let the doctor confirm or correct it *(insight 5)*.
- `discovery.accepted_vague_objection` (−) — moved on after a vague objection ("patients don't like it") without clarifying it *(insights 5–6)*.
All marked `"source": "proposed"` so you can tune the points.

### B. Unanchored-comparative detector for the rep's own claims — *small, measurable*
A pure function alongside `src/lib/hedging.ts`: flag rep turns containing comparatives/superlatives ("better", "more effective", "safer", "best", "the most…") with no stated comparison ("than…", "compared with…", "versus…"). Feed counts to the analyst the same way `hedgeMeasurements()` does, as a **review indicator, not a verdict**, backing `value_communication.unsupported_claim`. *(insight 14)*
⚠️ The Arabic list needs a native speaker's review, as was done for the Arabic hedge lists. I shouldn't invent it.

### C. "Precision Question" drill — *medium*
A new mode modelled on the Question Drill (`src/hooks/useQuestionDrill.ts`, `src/lib/voice-partner-questioning.ts`): the AI doctor says one vague line ("Patients don't tolerate it", "My decision is made", "Drug X is better"). The rep asks one follow-up, and a judge classifies it as `specifying` / `what_stops` / `checked_interpretation` / `argued` / `pitched` / `other`. The doctor rewards a specifying question with a concrete detail and stays vague otherwise. This mirrors the book's own exercise sequence *(insight 17)*. Needs EN + AR lines and i18n.

### D. Doctor persona speaks in realistic vague patterns — *medium, touches the AI doctor*
In `src/agents/doctor/behavior.ts` / `prompt.ts`: on harder difficulties, the doctor states objections as generalizations or nominalizations and only reveals the specific underlying need when asked a specifying question. This makes A and C meaningful inside full role-plays. It needs care so the doctor doesn't become artificially evasive.

### E. Debrief card: "Vague statements you let pass" — *medium*
In the coach/report: quote the doctor's vague line (verbatim, with turn number, following the analyst's evidence rules) and show the pattern plus one model question from the table above. Depends on A.

### F. Coach tips grounded in the book — *small, content only*
Short tips for the coach library: "Hold the pitch until the doctor's picture is complete" (p. 50), "Ask how, not why" (p. 56), "Ask 'what stops you?'" (pp. 50–51), "Separate what the doctor said from what you assumed" (pp. 58–59). Cite the book as the source of the *idea* and present the sales application as StyleShift's adaptation.

**My suggestion:** start with **A + B + F**. They're small and testable, they reuse existing pipelines, and they strengthen rules StyleShift already has. Do **C → D → E** after you've seen A's observations on real sessions.
